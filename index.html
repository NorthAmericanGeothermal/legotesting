Leveling the Peak

What to do when demand spikes and a machine can't keep up, shown with a toy-factory example.

1 · The setup

Molding makes Brick Bags at 0.1 hour each. It has 60 hours a week, so it can make at most 600 bags a week.

A holiday spike hits the finished sets in W5 and W6. Because MRP is just-in-time, it plans bags the week before they're needed, so the spike lands on W4.

W4 needs 80 hours against 60 available: 20 hours over. MRP doesn't check the machine. It plans what's needed and leaves the conflict for the planner.

3 · Why it works

You can't make 800 in W4, because only 600 fit. The other 200 have to be made in a different week. W3 has 20 spare hours right before the spike.

MRP will never build early on its own. A firm order is the planner overriding it: "release 600 bags in W3, no matter what."

  • W3 makes 600 bags: 60 hours, exactly full.
  • They arrive in W4, so 200 are already on the shelf.
  • MRP now only needs to start 600 in W4, not 800: 60 hours, exactly full.
4 · The trade

Planners have three tools. Each one costs something different.

Build earlyCosts stock. Average inventory rose from 168 to 188 in this example.
Add overtimeCosts labor money. Allowed here only up to 8 hours.
Ship lateCosts customer trust. Not allowed in this scenario.
Leveling means trading inventory for capacity: you carry extra stock for a week so the machine never has to exceed its limit.

Numbers come from the Tidewater Planning Desk practice factory: a simplified teaching model of the planning logic that ERP systems such as SAP and Kinaxis run.
